LESSON 6- Nutrition for Preschool and Primary Children

Nutritional Needs of Young Children

 

1.    A balanced diet

Early childhood is an important stage in physical growth and development. Unlike adults, young children do not have the ability to plan or choose appropriate food for themselves. Moreover, they are incapable of expressing their nutritional needs to their carers or the school based on their physical needs. Therefore, the school and the family have to plan daily meals for them based on scientific evidence as well as objective and professional recommendations, with the aim of helping them develop good eating habits and laying a solid foundation for their growth in the future.

Our energy needs vary with our race, age and other factors. The dietary habits and cultural backgrounds of groups would also affect the types of food to be recommended. Therefore, the dietary recommendations in this part are formulated with reference to the local Healthy Eating Food Pyramid (Figure 1). Kindergartens and child care centers may also refer to Table 1 for the recommended daily food intake for young children.

2. Nutrient distribution for different meals in one day

To meet the physiological needs of young children, most kindergartens and child care centers would provide catering service. Those with whole-day sessions even have to provide young children with breakfast, lunch as well as morning and afternoon snacks, implying that some young children may possibly have about two-thirds of their daily meals at school. Therefore, schools have to plan and provide a balanced and diversified diet for young children to obtain most of the nutrients required during school time every day, while helping them develop good eating habits since they are little.

 

Kindergartens and child care centers should pay attention to the nutrient distribution for main meals and snacks. Each main meal (including breakfast, lunch and dinner) should provide about one-third of the daily energy and nutrients required (see Table 3 for the recommended servings for main meals), while snacks should provide the rest (see Table 6 for the recommended servings for snacks). Schools may also determine the nutrient distribution for each school meal for young children appropriately by referring to Appendix 2 One-day Menu: Examples .

In addition, kindergartens and child care centers should also communicate with parents regarding the food consumption of their young children at school more often. This is to facilitate them in making adjustments on the food arrangements and servings for their young children at home, with the aim of meeting their daily nutritional needs.

 

Main Meals: Quality and Quantity

 

1.Good practices for preparing meals

To put healthy eating into practice, the school not only have to focus on providing a balanced and diversified diet, but also should:

◆ provide main meals at a regular time, with each meal scheduled 4 to 6 hours apart;

◆ provide grains in the greatest amount during main meals, followed by vegetables, with meat, fish, egg and alternatives the least;

◆ choose fresh and healthy ingredients more often, and avoid semi-products or processed foods containing added oil, salt or sugar;

◆ remove visible animal fat and skin before cooking;

◆ avoid using monosodium glutamate (MSG), or foods or seasonings high in fat, sodium or sugar when cooking; and limit the use of salt, soy sauce and oyster sauce due to their high sodium content; and

◆ adopt low-fat cooking methods (e.g. steaming, blanching, stir-frying with less oil) more often.

Snacks: Quality and Quantity

Growing rapidly and remaining highly active throughout the day, young children have a small stomach in general, thus they are often unable to fully obtain the nutrients required for supporting the body from the main meals. Hence, most kindergartens and child care centers serve snacks on a daily basis to make up the energy, nutrients and water that could not be consumed during main meals or were lost during activities.

Healthy snacks serve as a supplementary source of nutrients. However, choosing foods high in fat, sodium or sugar may result in obesity or affect health in ways such as spoiling the appetite for main meals and hindering the absorption of nutrients that the body needs. This would in turn affect growth and development.

 

1.    Principles of providing snacks

◆   Snacks should provide young children with nutrients that are beneficial to health but may not be fully obtained from main meals, such as dietary fibre, vitamin C, calcium.

◆   Diversified foods of 3 Low 1 High (i.e. low-fat, low-sodium (salt), low-sugar and high-dietary fibre) and without artificial sweeteners or caffeine should be served. This is to prevent young children from developing a sweet tooth and their sleep quality or learning performance from being adversely affected.

◆   Snacks should be served in a small amount (see Table 6) in order not to spoil young children s appetite for main meals.

◆   An interval of at least 1.5 to 2 hours should be maintained between snack time and mealtime.

◆   Snacks should only be served once between main meals, and young children should only be provided with water outside of mealtimes and snack times to reduce the risk of tooth decay.

2.    Recommended ingredients for snacks

Kindergartens and child care centers may refer to Table 4 recommended ingredients for healthy snacks when doing basic preparation for daily snacks. If there is a need to provide other types of food for whole-day classes during snack times to help young children achieve a balanced diet, they may refer to Table 4 other ingredients and the food examples in Appendix 3 Four-week Menu: An Example .

Half-day kindergartens and child care centers may not be able to provide young children with freshly-made foods every day due to various reasons (e.g. lacking adequate cooking equipment or having a tight school schedule). As a result, they may serve prepackaged foods such as biscuits or cakes as snacks instead. However, most biscuits and cakes contain more fat, salt or sugar than fresh foods, and thus are not healthy options for daily snacks.

Kindergartens and child care centers may refer to Table 4 recommended ingredients for healthy snacks to prepare easy-to-make and healthy snacks (e.g. wholemeal bread, bread rolls, fruit, cucumbers, cherry tomatoes, low-fat milk) for young children as far as possible. They should only consider prepackaged foods mentioned under Snacks to Choose in Moderation in Table 5 when necessary.

Healthy Birthday Parties

 

Birthday parties are celebration events held every month or every two months in most kindergartens and child care centers. They are also among the most favourite school activities of young children. However, most foods for birthday parties (e.g. sausages, potato chips, sweets, chocolate) are high in fat, sodium or sugar, with a lack of fruit and vegetables. In addition to not being beneficial to the health of young children, they may also lead young children to associate unhealthy foods with a happy and joyous mood, thereby adversely affecting the cultivation of healthy eating habits in them. To help young children develop good eating habits, kindergartens and child care centers may take this opportunity to let young children approach and enjoy nutritious foods more often. The aim is to enhance their understanding of these foods so that they can accept them more easily.

 

1.     Common foods for a birthday party and the latent problems

Let us first learn about the common foods for celebration and their effects on our bodies.

 

 

Common food for a birthday party

Latent problem

 

Deep-fried fish

fillet (deep-fried

foods)

Deep-frying increases the fat and energy content of the food. The fat content of every 100 g of deep-fried fish fillet is 7 times higher than that of steamed fish fillet of a comparable weight, with twice as much as the energy content.

 

Sausages

A lot of sodium (salt) is added during food processing. Frequent consumption may result in hypertension. The amount of salt that three sausages (about 45 g each) contain already exceeds the daily recommended intake for a young child.

 

Potato chips

They are high in energy, fat and salt, with about 60% of their energy coming from fat. The amount of salt that a pack of potato chips (about 55 g) contains constitutes nearly one-third of the daily recommended intake for a young child.

 

Sweets

About 75% of their energy comes from sugar. The amount of sugar that a pack of sweets (about 50 g) contains already exceeds the daily recommended intake for a young child per day. Frequent consumption of foods high in sugar also increases the risk of tooth decay.

 

Chocolate

It is high in energy, fat and sugar, with over 85% of its energy coming from fat and sugar. The energy content of a pack of milk chocolate (about 34 g, small-sized) is higher than that of half a bowl of rice. In comparison, while dark chocolate contains 50% less sugar, its fat content is about 1.5 times higher.

Concentrated fruit-flavoured drinks/cordials

If you take a closer look at the ingredients of most beverages on their packaging, it is not difficult to discover that these beverages contain mainly water and sugar with only little juice and nutrients. Serving these beverages to young children is no different from giving them sugar and water .

Notes on Healthy Cooking

 

1.     Healthy cooking methods

Healthy cooking methods not only can bring out the umami of the food, but also can reduce the fat content of a dish. Below are some healthy cooking methods and cooking tips:

Recommended cooking method

 

Cooking tip

 

Steaming

(no oil is needed)

 

  Egg: Apart from simply steaming eggs, different vegetables can also be added to the egg mixture to increase the opportunities for young children to eat vegetables.

  Meat and poultry: Remove the fat and skin before steaming.

  Seafood: Steaming can preserve the umami of seafood. However, parts with bones should be removed before being served to young children.

  Vegetables: Steaming can preserve water-soluble vitamins in the vegetables which are easily lost. Pumpkins, tomatoes, radishes and eggplants are all

appropriate options.

 

Blanching, boiling

 

  Vegetables: To minimise the loss of nutrients during cooking, boiling water must be used, with the water level just enough to cover the vegetables. The cooking time has to be appropriate. Cook stems before leaves.

 

Grilling, baking

 

  Most foods, including meat, gourds and root vegetables (e.g. pumpkins, eggplants, zucchinis, carrots) are suitable for grilling and baking.

 

Stir-frying with less oil

 

  Use less oil to prevent young children from consuming an excessive amount of fat, which would in turn increase the risk of obesity.

  Reduce the use of oil by using a non-stick wok or stir-frying food after blanching. No more than 2 teaspoons of oil per person per main meal should be used when preparing food (including the amount of oil used when handling and cooking food).

 

 

2.     Recommendations on the use of oil

It is recommended that no more than 2 teaspoons of oil per person per main meal should be used when handling and cooking food. Schools may choose vegetable oils (e.g. peanut oil, canola oil, corn oil, olive oil), which are healthier, for cooking.

 

Avoid using fats and oils rich in saturated fat such as butter, lard, palm oil, coconut oil, coconut cream, cream when preparing meals for young children. When buying vegetable oils , read the lists of ingredients on the packaging of the products to ensure that they do not contain the aforesaid fats and oils

 

3.     Recommendations on the use of seasonings

 

Sodium

Excessive intake of sodium in the long term may lead to chronic diseases such as hypertension and heart diseases. Therefore, while processed or cured foods, which are high in sodium, should be chosen less often, the amount of salt and seasonings of a high sodium content should also be limited when cooking for young children. Kindergartens and child care centers may refer to the following recommendations on the use of seasonings:

 

◆   Choose natural ingredients, spices and herbs such as ginger, garlic, spring onions, onions, a trace amount of pepper, lemons, oranges, vinegar.

◆   Make a gravy with fresh fruit and vegetables. Some examples are tomato sauce, pumpkin sauce, sweet corn sauce, spinach sauce.

◆   Incorporating fresh fruit into the dishes (e.g. Stir-fried Pork Slices with Dragon Fruit , Steamed Meat Patty with Apple Dices ) to enhance the colour, aroma and taste.

 

Sugar

 

◆   The World Health Organization recommends that young children s intake of sugar (free sugars)* should be less than 10% of their total daily energy intake. Taking a 4-year-old boy who consumes 1300 kcal of energy per day as an example, his daily intake of free sugars should be less than 32.5 g (about 6.5 teaspoons). For more information, please visit the following webpage: www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/free-sugars-adults-ncds.

◆   Use less sugar as far as possible when seasoning, cooking or making sweet soup by yourself. Avoid adding extra sugars to food and reduce the amount of sugars used in recipes.

◆   Check whether the ingredients already contain sugar (some examples of those containing sugar are apples, pears, papayas, dates and raisins) beforehand to decide whether extra sugar is necessary.
